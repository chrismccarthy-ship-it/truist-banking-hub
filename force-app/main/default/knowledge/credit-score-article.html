Understanding Your Credit Score

Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in your financial life. Whether you're applying for a mortgage, a car loan, or a new credit card, lenders use this three-digit number to decide whether to lend to you — and at what interest rate. Here's everything you need to know.

What Is a Credit Score?

A credit score is a numerical summary of your creditworthiness — a snapshot of how reliably you've managed debt in the past. Scores typically range from 300 to 850. The higher your score, the more financially trustworthy you appear to lenders.

The most widely used scoring model is the FICO® Score, although VantageScore is also used by many lenders. Most major lenders rely on scores generated by the three national credit bureaus:

  • Equifax
  • Experian
  • TransUnion

Because each bureau may have slightly different data on file, your score can vary a few points between them.

What Is a Credit Score Used For?

Lenders, landlords, insurers, and even some employers use credit scores to make important decisions about you:

  • Loan & Credit Card Applications — Determines if you qualify and what interest rate you'll receive.
  • Mortgage Approvals — A higher score can save you tens of thousands of dollars over the life of a home loan.
  • Auto Loans — Impacts both approval odds and the APR offered.
  • Renting an Apartment — Many landlords pull your credit as part of the application process.
  • Insurance Premiums — Some states allow insurers to use credit-based insurance scores to set premiums.
  • Utility Deposits — Utility companies may require a deposit if your score is low.
  • Employment Screening — Certain employers, especially in finance or government roles, may review your credit history.

The Five Factors That Determine Your Score

FICO® scores are calculated using five weighted categories. Understanding each one can help you take targeted steps to improve your score.

35%

Payment History

The single biggest factor. Lenders want to know: do you pay on time? Even one late payment can noticeably lower your score. Payment history includes credit cards, installment loans, mortgages, and some utility accounts. Bankruptcies and collections also appear here.

Tip: Set up automatic minimum payments to prevent missed due dates.

30%

Amounts Owed (Credit Utilization)

This measures how much of your available revolving credit you're currently using. A utilization rate above 30% can start to hurt your score. Carrying a $3,000 balance on a card with a $10,000 limit = 30% utilization.

Tip: Aim to keep utilization below 30% — ideally under 10% for the best scores.

15%

Length of Credit History

Longer credit histories generally lead to higher scores. This factor considers the age of your oldest account, the age of your newest account, and the average age of all accounts. Closing old accounts can inadvertently reduce your average account age.

Tip: Keep older accounts open, even if you rarely use them.

10%

Credit Mix

Having a variety of credit types — such as credit cards, installment loans, auto loans, and mortgages — shows lenders you can manage different kinds of debt responsibly.

Tip: Don't open accounts just to diversify, but be aware that a healthy mix can help.

10%

New Credit (Hard Inquiries)

Applying for new credit triggers a "hard inquiry," which can temporarily lower your score by a few points. Multiple applications in a short window can signal financial stress. Rate shopping for mortgages or auto loans within a short period is typically treated as a single inquiry.

Tip: Only apply for new credit when you genuinely need it.

How to Improve Your Credit Score

  1. Pay every bill on time, every time. Even partial payments are better than none — but pay at least the minimum by the due date.
  2. Pay down revolving balances. Reducing your credit card balances is one of the fastest ways to see a score improvement.
  3. Don't close old accounts. Keeping them open preserves your credit age and available credit limit.
  4. Check your credit reports for errors. You're entitled to free annual reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com. Dispute inaccuracies — they do happen.
  5. Limit new credit applications. Only apply when necessary, and space out applications over time.
  6. Consider a secured credit card if you're building credit from scratch or recovering from past issues. Use it lightly and pay it off monthly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does checking my own credit score hurt it?

No. Checking your own credit score is a "soft inquiry" and has no impact on your score. Only hard inquiries — triggered when a lender reviews your credit for a lending decision — can temporarily lower your score.

How long does it take to improve a bad credit score?

It depends on what's hurting your score. Late payments remain on your report for seven years, but their impact diminishes over time. With consistent on-time payments and lower utilization, many people see meaningful improvement within 6–12 months.

Will getting a Truist credit card affect my credit score?

Applying for any new credit card triggers a hard inquiry, which may temporarily lower your score by a few points. However, if approved and used responsibly — paying on time and keeping utilization low — a new card can ultimately help your score over time.

Does being an authorized user on someone else's account affect my score?

Yes. Being added as an authorized user on an account with a positive history (on-time payments, low utilization) can help build your credit. Conversely, being added to a poorly managed account can hurt your score.
